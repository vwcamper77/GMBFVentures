Verified closeout: why “done” should start the review

Contractor completion and client acceptance are different events. Recording them separately is the simplest way to make defect closeout trustworthy at handover.

Why closeout goes wrong

Most defect lists start well. Someone walks the site, records what they see and issues the list to the contractor. The difficulty comes later, when items start coming back as “done”.

On many projects, that single word does two jobs. It records that the contractor believes the work is complete, and it is often treated as though the client has accepted it. Those are different events, made by different people, for different reasons. When one status stands for both, the register stops being a reliable record of what has actually been accepted.

Independent guidance points the same way. The New Homes Quality Board’s guide for homeowners advises keeping “a detailed log of any potential snags”, including the date each was reported and a description, so that progress can be tracked.1 The government’s project delivery standard, GovS 002, includes among its principles that the transition to operations is planned, “with ongoing operational responsibilities agreed and accepted”.2 Acceptance, in other words, is a deliberate step, not a by-product of a contractor marking something complete.

Keep completion and acceptance separate

SnagSort’s own closeout guide, which we publish, puts the principle simply: “done” is the start of the review. A contractor submits work as ready for inspection, and an authorised person records whether to accept it or return it.3

“Done” is the start of the review.SnagSort practical guide (GMBF’s own product content)

In practice, that means a register with at least three distinct states after the item is issued:

Defect states and what each records
StateWho moves it thereWhat it records
OpenInspector or project teamAn observed defect, with location, description and photographs
Ready for inspectionResponsible contractorA claim that the work is complete, with the contractor’s evidence
ClosedAuthorised reviewerThe acceptance decision: who accepted it, when, and on what evidence

“Ready for inspection” then becomes a review queue that someone owns, rather than a status that quietly counts as finished.

Return with a reason

When the reviewer is not satisfied, the item should go back with a specific reason, not a generic rejection. Our SnagSort guide recommends returning it with a specific reason and next action, and preserving the previous response rather than overwriting it, so the history of the item survives.3

A register is only as trustworthy as the history it keeps.

That history is what allows a project team to answer the questions that come up later: why was this accepted, who decided, and what did they see at the time?

Evidence that supports the decision

Photographs carry much of the weight in defect management, but only if they show enough. Our guidance on photographs suggests pairing a context shot that shows where the defect is with a detail shot that shows what is wrong, and makes a point that is easy to miss: submitted photographs mean there is evidence to review, but they do not by themselves record acceptance.4

The same applies to the written description. A clear description covers three things: where the defect is, what was observed, and what action is needed. Where software suggests the wording, treat it as a draft to check before it is used.5

Checklist for each defectLocation a stranger could find · what was observed · the action required · a context photograph and a detail photograph · the responsible party.

Fitting it into the inspection

None of this needs a complicated process. A snagging inspection can be organised in four stages: capture, structure, issue and close.6 Verified closeout simply makes the last stage explicit. For new homes covered by the New Homes Quality Code, the NHQB guide says developers should keep homeowners updated and resolve snags and defects within 30 days wherever possible. Emergencies should be resolved within hours.1 Clear status is what makes that kind of commitment trackable.

What should change on a project

  • Clearer status. Everyone can see the difference between items the contractor says are complete and items the client has accepted.
  • Owned review. “Ready for inspection” is a queue with a named reviewer, not a grey area.
  • Defensible handover. Each closed item carries the decision, the decision-maker and the evidence.
  • Less rework on the register. Returned items keep their history, so nobody has to reconstruct what happened from email.

These are the intended process benefits, not measured results, and they do not depend on any one product. They can be applied with any register that keeps completion and acceptance apart.
