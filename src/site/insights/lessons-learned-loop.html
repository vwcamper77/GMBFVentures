Closing the lessons learned loop

UK government project delivery guidance expects learning throughout the life cycle. Here is what a lesson record that leads to action looks like, and where software fits.

The end-of-project problem

Lessons learned are one of the most familiar ideas in project delivery, and one of the least satisfying in practice. Lessons are often gathered at the end of a project, written into a report and filed. By the time the next project starts, the people who would benefit are rarely the people who read it.

The Teal Book is direct about this: “Recording a lesson in a register or adding to existing documentation does not mean people will read it.”1 A lesson captured without an owner, an action or a route back into the next project has nowhere to go.

What UK guidance expects

UK government project delivery guidance treats learning as continuous rather than a closing activity. The Government Functional Standard for project delivery, GovS 002 (version 2.1, September 2025), lists among its principles that “experience and lessons are captured, shared and used to promote future performance improvement”.2

Clause 8.10, Learning from experience, says that at the start of the work, those involved and key stakeholders should identify and apply relevant lessons from previous experience when planning the work, and that “throughout the life cycle, lessons should be continually captured, evaluated and action should be taken”. It also expects the owners of standards, processes, methods, guidance, tools and training to update their knowledge sources and share learning as appropriate.2 Clause 6.4.5, on initiating a programme or project, requires the delivery strategy and plan to take into account lessons learned from previous, relevant work.2

The Teal Book’s chapter on learning from experience goes further on practice. It asks teams to record lessons in a lessons register, review them at set points, and not to rely solely on set-piece workshops at the end of a phase. It makes the senior responsible owner accountable for learning from experience in a programme or project.1

“Learning from experience should happen throughout the life cycle of the work, not just at the end.”The Teal Book, Chapter 38: Learning from experience

A lesson record that leads to action

The Teal Book’s description of a lesson record is a useful test for any register. Each lesson should combine four things:1

The four parts of a lesson record
ElementThe question it answers
What happenedWhat threat was avoided, issue occurred or opportunity exploited?
ImpactWhat was the effect of what happened?
What was learnedWhat is the implication for future work?
Action takenWhat was the response to the lesson for the current work?

A record without the last row is an observation. A record with it is a lesson.

Five steps around the loop

Putting the guidance into practice, we draw the lessons process as a loop with five steps. This is our own synthesis, not a government model. The aim is that every lesson either changes something or is consciously set aside.

  1. Capture what happened, close to when it happened.
  2. Review its impact with the people involved.
  3. Confirm what was learned, so the record reflects an agreed view rather than one person’s note.
  4. Act by assigning an owner and recording the change made.
  5. Embed it in the next project’s start-up, standards or procurement, which is where GovS 002 expects relevant lessons to be applied.2

Where software helps, and where it does not

Software can make the loop easier to run. It can prompt for the four elements, keep lessons searchable and show which ones are still waiting for review. AI can help by summarising a long discussion or suggesting similar lessons from earlier projects.

Software can suggest a lesson. Only people can agree one.

What software should not do is decide what was learned. A suggested summary is a draft until someone with knowledge of the project agrees it. That agreement step is what turns a pile of notes into a register people trust.

Questions for sponsors

  • Where are lessons from previous projects considered at the start of this one?
  • Who owns the lessons register, and who reviews new entries?
  • How many lessons have a recorded action and owner?
  • How does a lesson from this project reach the next business case?
